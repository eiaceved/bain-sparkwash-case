01

How a Bain-style case works

Bain cases often look like real client work, and private equity deals are a big part of that. You judge whether a business is worth buying: is the market good, is the company strong, and does the price leave room for a good return?

Put simply, they want clear structure, accurate math said out loud, and a practical, decisive answer. Formats vary by office, so confirm the details with your recruiter.

How the interview runs

Based on public prep guides

  • Most regions use an online test before interviews.
  • First-round cases are often more standard and interviewer-led. Later rounds with partners are often candidate-led.
  • Some offices add a written case: a data packet you analyze, then present.
  • Each interview usually pairs a case with a short experience interview.

What they look for

Practical and decisive

  • A clear structure for "should we buy this?"
  • Math done accurately, out loud.
  • A firm answer: buy, walk away, or bid lower.
  • Being easy to work with: a thinking partner, not a lecturer.

What the case feels like

  • Market. Is this a good market to be in, and is it getting more crowded?
  • Company. Is this business better than its rivals?
  • Value creation. How would the buyer grow profit while it owns the company?
  • Returns. At this price, does the buyer make enough when it sells?
  • Decision. Buy, walk away, or bid lower, and say why.

The due diligence path

  1. MarketGrowing? Crowded?
  2. CompanyBetter than rivals?
  3. Value planHow profit grows.
  4. ReturnsWorth the price?

The classic path for any "should we buy it?" case

Ten terms to drop naturally

Private equity (PE)

Firms that buy companies with investors' money, improve them, and sell them, usually within 3 to 7 years.

Due diligence

The deep check a buyer does before buying: the market, the company, the numbers, and the risks.

EBITDA

Roughly, the profit a business makes from its operations. Deals are usually priced as a multiple of it.

Entry and exit multiple

The price paid, and later received, as a multiple of EBITDA. If you sell at a lower multiple than you paid, returns shrink.

MOIC

Multiple on invested capital: money out divided by money in. Put in 100, get back 200, and that's 2 times.

IRR

The yearly rate of return. Doubling your money in 5 years works out to about 15 percent a year.

Buy-and-build

Growing a company by opening or buying more locations, then selling the bigger company for more.

Recurring revenue

Money that comes in every month, like memberships. Investors pay more for it because it's predictable.

Churn

The share of members who cancel. 4 percent a month means about 40 percent leave over a year.

Cohort

A group that started at the same time, like all sites opened in 2022. Compare cohorts at the same age to see if newer ones are weaker.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is Ridgeline Capital, a private equity firm. It's thinking about buying SparkWash, a chain of 50 express car washes, where drivers stay in the car while it rolls through an automated tunnel.

SparkWash makes about 90 million dollars in revenue and about 35 million dollars of EBITDA, which is roughly its operating profit. About 60 percent of its revenue comes from members who pay a monthly fee for unlimited washes. The owners are asking 525 million dollars.

Ridgeline would own SparkWash for about five years, then sell it. Ridgeline's partners have asked us: should they buy SparkWash at 525 million dollars?

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So Ridgeline wants to know whether SparkWash is worth buying at 525 million, given it plans to own it for about five years and then sell. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes. What else do you need to know?" Your best next question is what return Ridgeline needs. Without it, you can't say whether the price is too high.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What return does Ridgeline need?
    At least 2 times its money over five years, which is about 15 percent a year.
  • How would Ridgeline grow SparkWash?
    Management's plan is to open 10 new sites a year, reaching 100 sites by the time Ridgeline sells.
  • What does a new site cost?
    About 4 million dollars. Management says SparkWash's own cash flow can pay for them.
  • What do car wash chains sell for?
    Recent deals have been around 12 to 16 times EBITDA. Some investors worry prices have run too high.
  • Who are the competitors?
    Other express chains, many also backed by private equity, plus older gas station washes.

Skip questions that don't change your approach, like what soap SparkWash uses. Two or three sharp questions beat six generic ones.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Great. How would you structure the due diligence?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch three or four buckets, then walk the interviewer through them out loud.

Strong framework

Should Ridgeline buy SparkWash for $525M?
1Market
  • Size and growth of car washing
  • Shift to memberships
  • How crowded areas are getting
2Company
  • Site profits and members
  • Strength vs. rivals
  • Track record on new sites
3Value creation
  • New sites: how many, how good
  • Price and membership growth
  • Cost savings
4Returns and risks
  • Entry price vs. exit value
  • Money multiple vs. 2× target
  • What could go wrong

Say it like this

"I'd treat this as a due diligence in four parts. First, the market: is car washing growing, and how crowded is it getting? Second, the company: are SparkWash's sites strong compared with rivals? Third, value creation: how Ridgeline would grow profit, mainly through new sites. Fourth, returns: what SparkWash is worth when Ridgeline sells, and whether that beats the 2 times target at 525 million. My early hypothesis is that it's a good business at a full price, so I'd start with the market and how new sites are performing."

Why it works

  • It's the standard private equity path, tailored to this deal: memberships and new sites.
  • It ends at returns, which is the question the partners actually asked.
  • It states a hypothesis and a place to start, which hands the interviewer a natural next step.

If you blank, "market, company, value creation, returns" works for almost any private equity case.

05

Exhibit 1: the market and the business

Interviewer

Let's start there. Here are six years of market and company data. What stands out?

Which finding should you lead with?

Members now bring in 60 percent of revenue, which investors like because it's steady. But express sites in SparkWash's states went from 400 to 720, up 80 percent. Washes per site peaked in 2022 and are falling, and the share of members canceling each month rose from 3 percent to over 4. That's what a crowding market looks like. Higher prices per wash are hiding it for now.

Then

Say the insight out loud, tie it to the deal, and ask for the next piece of data.

Say it like this

"Memberships are a real strength: 60 percent of revenue is recurring. But the market is getting crowded. Rival sites are up 80 percent, washes per site have been falling since 2022, and monthly cancellations are up to about 4 percent. That matters because the plan depends on opening 50 new sites into this market. Can I see how SparkWash's newer sites are doing compared with its older ones?"

Notice the pattern: insight, then so-what for the deal, then a hypothesis-driven ask. That ask leads the interviewer to Exhibit 2.

06

Exhibit 2: are new sites as good as old ones?

Interviewer

Good instinct. Here's each group of sites, compared at the same age. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

Compare like with like: every number here is profit in a site's second full year. Sites opened in 2015 to 2017 earned 650 thousand at that age. Sites opened in 2022 and 2023 earned only 450 thousand, about 30 percent less. That fits Exhibit 1: newer sites open into more crowded areas. If the next 50 sites follow the trend, they may reach about 500 thousand, not 700.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences.

Say it like this

"The business is good: steady membership revenue and strong older sites. But each newer group of sites starts weaker, and the newest earn about 30 percent less than the oldest did at the same age, because the market is crowding. So I'd stress-test the plan: if the 50 new sites reach 500 thousand instead of 700, exit EBITDA drops by 10 million, so let me run the returns both ways."

Returns math: does the price work?

This is the private equity version of a sizing question: entry price, exit value, and the money multiple. Say every step out loud.

Case assumptions. To keep the math simple, Ridgeline pays all cash and new sites are paid for from SparkWash's own cash flow. The exit multiple assumes prices cool from today's highs.

Your move

Say the formula first: money multiple equals exit value divided by entry price. Then work backward to the most Ridgeline should pay.

  1. 1 · Entry price$35M × 15$525M
  2. 2 · Exit EBITDA, plan100 sites × $700K$70M
  3. 3 · Exit value$70M × 12. At 15× it would be $1,050M$840M
  4. 4 · Money multiple at the ask$840M ÷ $525M. About 10% a year, below the 2× target1.6×
  5. 5 · Most Ridgeline should pay$840M ÷ 2 ≈ 12× today's EBITDA$420M
  6. 6 · Downside check$35M + 50 × $500K = $60M. × 12 = $720M. At a $400M bid1.8×

Quick check: what's the money multiple if Ridgeline pays 525 million and sells for 840 million?

840 divided by 525 is 1.6. Quick way to check: 525 times 1.6 is 525 plus 315, which is 840. Everyday version: buy a used car for 5,000 dollars, sell it for 8,000, and you've made 1.6 times your money.

At $525M = 1.6× Most to pay for 2× ≈ $420M Downside at $400M = 1.8×
Then

Summarize the math in two sentences, and add one sanity check.

Say it like this

"At the 525 million ask, Ridgeline gets back about 840 million in five years, only 1.6 times its money, or about 10 percent a year, well short of the 2 times target. To hit 2 times, it shouldn't pay more than about 420 million, roughly 12 times today's EBITDA. And if new sites come in weaker, as Exhibit 2 suggests, even a 400 million bid only returns 1.8 times."

If the interviewer asks, "What if the exit multiple stays at 15?": "Then the exit value is about 1,050 million and the asking price returns exactly 2 times. But that bets buyers will still pay top prices in five years, in a market that's getting crowded. I wouldn't pay for that."

08

Score the diligence risks

Interviewer

Here are the risks the team has logged. Score each for likelihood and impact, from 1 to 5, with one reason for each. Which three would you dig into before Ridgeline bids?

Your move

Score each risk out loud with a one-line reason, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd dig into three before bidding. First, new-site performance, because the whole plan rests on 50 new sites and the newest ones are already weaker. Second, competition near existing sites, since cancellations are rising. Third, the exit multiple: buying at 15 times and selling at 12 takes a big bite out of the return. Weather matters less, because members pay every month, rain or shine."

    Which three come first?

    R1 scores highest and drives the whole plan. R2 is already showing up in the data. R3 is the classic private equity risk: paying a high multiple and selling at a lower one. R4 matters less because memberships smooth out bad weather. R5 can delay the plan but doesn't break it. R6 is minor for a business with few staff.

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The partners just walked in. You have 60 seconds. Should Ridgeline buy SparkWash?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

    Strong answer

    Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

    "Ridgeline shouldn't pay 525 million. I'd bid about 400 million, roughly 11 times EBITDA, and walk away above 420 million."

    "First, the business is good. 60 percent of revenue is recurring membership, and older sites earn about 900 thousand dollars each."

    "Second, the price is too high for the return. At 525 million, Ridgeline gets about 1.6 times its money in five years, short of its 2 times target. At 400 million, the plan returns about 2.1 times."

    "Third, growth is getting harder. Rival sites are up 80 percent, cancellations are rising, and newer sites earn about 30 percent less than older ones did at the same age. If that continues, even 400 million returns about 1.8 times."

    "The main risk is losing the deal to a buyer willing to pay more. That's acceptable: paying 15 times for a business in a crowding market is how returns get hurt. Next steps: visit the newest sites and their local rivals, rebuild the new-site plan site by site, and model returns at exit multiples from 10 to 15 times."

    The diligence plan before bidding

    Weeks 1–2Market
    • Map every rival site near SparkWash's locations.
    • Survey members on why they cancel.
    Weeks 3–4Company
    • Rebuild new-site forecasts, site by site.
    • Review the 50 planned locations for crowding.
    Weeks 5–6Price
    • Model returns at exit multiples of 10 to 15 times.
    • Set the walk-away price, then bid.

    Indicators to track after buying

    • EBITDA per new site in year 2. Target: 550 thousand dollars or more.
    • Members canceling per month. Target: below 3.5 percent.
    • Washes per site per day. Target: steady at about 300.
    • New sites opened. Target: 10 a year.

    Market sizing: car washes in one city

    Interviewer

    How many express car washes can a metro area of 2 million people support?

    Your move

    Work from demand to supply: people, cars, washes a year, then divide by what one site can handle. Say each assumption out loud.

    Market growth: why PE loves car washes

    Interviewer

    Why have private equity firms been buying car washes, and will it last?

    Your move

    Split growth into more customers and more spend per customer, then list drivers and headwinds.

    Strong answer

    Market growth = more customers × more spend per customer
    Memberships

    Monthly plans turn car washes into steady, recurring revenue.

    Many small owners

    Lots of single-site owners to buy and combine into a chain.

    Automation

    A tunnel site runs with only a few staff.

    Convenience

    A wash in a few minutes without leaving the car.

    Headwinds

    • Everyone is building, so areas get crowded fast.
    • Higher building costs and interest rates make new sites less profitable.
    • If buyers keep paying top prices now, future sale prices may fall.

    Quick math

    If membership revenue grows 20 percent a year, how long until it doubles? Use the rule of 72: 72 divided by 20 is about 3.6 years.

    "The model is attractive, which is exactly why it's getting crowded. Future returns will depend on picking sites well and paying a sensible price, not just on building more."
